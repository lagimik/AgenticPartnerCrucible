## Headline findings (MDDR 2026)

- Government agencies and services were the sector most impacted by cyber
  threats in 2026: **27% of observed activity**, up from 17% in 2025.
- Governments are also the most frequently targeted sector for **nation-state
  activity**.
- **Dwell time** (gap between attacker access and detection) increased across
  multiple sectors in 2026, even though organizations responded faster once
  an intrusion was identified — detection, not response speed, is the
  lagging capability.
- **Phishing** accounted for 23% of observed intrusions in 2026, up from 7%
  in 2025 — compromised identities remain a primary entry point.
- **52.2%** of intrusions involving valid (compromised) accounts resulted in
  additional credential theft — a single foothold readily expands.
- Publicly disclosed software vulnerabilities (CVEs) are projected to reach
  a record **72,000 in 2026**; the gap from vulnerability discovery to
  active weaponization can be well under 24 hours.
- Sector impact distribution (2026): government agencies/services 27%,
  information technology 17%, research and academia 14%.
- Nation-state targeting distribution: IT and research/academia 20% each,
  government and think tanks/NGOs 10% each, transportation 7%,
  manufacturing 5%, other sectors 28%.
- Geographic impact (Jan–Jun 2026): United States 25.5% of total impact,
  Israel 7.6%, Ukraine 4.8%, Taiwan 3.9%, United Kingdom 3.8%, United Arab
  Emirates 2.8%, India and Japan 2.5% each, Canada 1.8%, Germany 1.7%,
  Australia and Philippines 1.5% each.
- SLED (state/local/education) and critical-infrastructure attack
  distribution (Nov 2025–Apr 2026): research and academia 38%, transportation
  systems 22%, government agencies/services 13%, communications
  infrastructure 11%, critical manufacturing 7%, energy 4%, government
  facilities 4%, chemicals 1%, water/wastewater systems 1%, emergency
  services 1%.

## Five priorities for government resilience

1. **Prepare for a faster threat environment.** AI is compressing the
   window between vulnerability discovery and weaponization. Resilience
   requires rapid information-gathering/assessment, clear pre-defined
   responsibilities, and trusted cross-institution relationships
   established *before* an incident, not during one.
2. **Build security into the AI ecosystem.** Treat AI security as a
   resilience challenge across infrastructure, data, models, applications,
   suppliers, and governance — not a narrow technical add-on. Promote
   secure-by-design practices, testing/evaluation, supply-chain
   protections, transparency, accountability, and international
   cooperation. Explicit goal: integrate AI security into existing
   critical-infrastructure/national-resilience efforts rather than create a
   separate AI security agenda.
3. **Plan for incidents to spread.** Criminal and nation-state actors
   increasingly share entry points (compromised identities, exposed
   applications, social engineering, legitimate admin tools), so initial
   attribution is often unclear. Assess incidents by where they could lead,
   not just how they began; response plans must account for suppliers,
   partners, and service providers, since attacks cross organizational,
   sectoral, and national boundaries.
4. **Enable timely, two-way public-private information sharing.** No
   single institution sees the full picture of interconnected criminal
   infrastructure. Sharing must be bidirectional — government returning
   actionable intelligence/guidance/warnings to industry, not just
   receiving reports — through trusted channels that respect legal/privacy
   requirements. Policy levers: protections for good-faith sharing, common
   anonymization standards, investment in shared threat-intelligence/
   detection capability, and follow-through into investigations/disruption/
   accountability.
5. **Prepare essential services to operate through disruption.** Resilience
   requires understanding the broader ecosystem (transportation,
   communications, schools/universities, critical infrastructure operators)
   supporting government functions, not just government systems themselves.
   Planning documents alone are insufficient — regular tabletop exercises
   across policymakers, operational leaders, law enforcement, critical
   infrastructure operators, and private-sector partners expose gaps before
   a real crisis. Microsoft's Advancing Regional Cybersecurity (ARC)
   program (recent example: Kenya) is cited as an illustration of
   scenario-based, cross-sector preparedness work. Shared-service models can
   extend security/response capability to local governments/public
   institutions that cannot build it independently.

## Framing

Security in the AI era is framed as no longer about preventing individual
intrusions but about institutions operating effectively when risks are
interconnected, threats move faster, and attackers stay hidden longer.
Resilience is reframed from "recovering from an attack" to "containing
harm, adapting quickly, and continuing to deliver critical services" as
incidents move faster, spread further, and affect more organizations.
